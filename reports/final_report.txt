Used Car Value Retention

Rovena Nako | October 23, 2026

1. Project description

This project predicts how much of its original value a used car keeps. Value retention = listing price / original MSRP. For example, 0.60 means the car is listed for 60% of its original price.

2. Data collection

The data was scraped from CarGurus and iSeeCars with Requests and BeautifulSoup. Original MSRP came from CarGurus Research tables. The raw files save the page links and collection dates.

Source | Raw rows | Unique before merge | Final rows
CarGurus | 1824 | 887 | 884
iSeeCars | 1080 | 736 | 441
The final dataset has 1,325 unique cars from 2015-2025. It includes Toyota (Camry, Corolla, RAV4), Honda (Accord, Civic, CR-V), and Ford (Fusion, Escape, Explorer). Fusion years end at 2020. Each source had more than 500 unique valid listings before merging.

Collected fields include VIN, listing ID, make, model, year, trim, price, mileage, body type, color, title and location. This meets the requirement of at least ten original attributes.

3. Cleaning and combining the data

Column names and formats were made consistent. Prices use dollars and mileage uses miles. The code checks VINs and keeps prices from $1,000 to $150,000 and mileage from 0 to 500,000. Duplicate VINs are removed. If both sites list the same car, the CarGurus row is kept because it has more details.

Step | Rows left

Selected makes, models and years 2015-2025 | 2420

MSRP is matched by make, model, year and trim. Unclear matches are removed instead of guessing. Missing numbers are filled with the training median and missing categories with 'unknown' during training. The saved dataset still shows missing values.

4. Feature engineering

Vehicle age = collection year - model year. Miles per year is calculated for exploration, but the models use age and mileage separately. The target is listing price / MSRP.

Text features check for leather and navigation in equipment descriptions: 154 leather and 253 navigation mentions. Text is available for 881 cars. These are short phrases, not full seller paragraphs. Missing text stays unknown, with a flag for description availability.

One-hot encoding turns categories into columns. The training data has 561 cities and 91 trims. Values appearing fewer than ten times are grouped together. This saves columns but loses rare-category details.

5. Models

All three models use the same 80/20 split: 1060 training cars and 265 test cars. The split is balanced by make/model and uses random_state=42. Three-fold cross-validation on training data chooses the best model. Missing-value filling, scaling and encoding are learned only from training rows.

Model | Settings
Ridge regression | alpha=10
Random forest | 200 trees, min_samples_leaf=3, max_features=0.8
Gradient boosting | 150 trees, learning_rate=0.05, max_depth=3, min_samples_leaf=5
Both tree models use seed 42. Inputs are age, mileage, MSRP, text flags, make, model, trim, body type, color, city and source. Numbers are standardized. Price and retention are excluded to avoid giving away the answer.

6. Results

Model | CV RMSE | Test MAE | Test RMSE | Test R²
Ridge regression | 0.0619 | 0.0474 | 0.0612 | 0.9091
Random forest | 0.0651 | 0.0507 | 0.0649 | 0.8979
Gradient boosting | 0.0615 | 0.0472 | 0.0602 | 0.9122
Lower MAE and RMSE are better. Higher R² is better; it is not accuracy. Gradient boosting had the lowest training CV error. Its test MAE of 0.0472 means an average error of about 4.72 percentage points of original value. Ridge was close, so boosting may not always be the best on new data.

8. Depreciation and feature ranking

Feature importance was measured by shuffling one input and checking how much worse the predictions became. This was repeated ten times. A bigger increase in error means the feature mattered more to the model.

Figure 5. Mileage, make and age were the main predictors. Bars show the average change in RMSE; error lines show variation across shuffles.

Higher mileage and older age generally went with lower value retention. Make also mattered, but the brands have different mixes of age, mileage and models. The results do not prove that the brand alone caused the difference.

MSRP and city added some information. Leather and navigation mentions had little importance. Trim and MSRP may already capture these details, and many descriptions were missing. Related inputs can share importance, so the ranking is not exact proof of what causes depreciation.

Figure 6. The model's average prediction when age or mileage changes. These show model patterns, not how one specific car will lose value.

9. Conclusion

The project used 1,325 unique cars from two websites and compared three models. Gradient boosting performed best in this experiment, with ridge close behind. Mileage, make and age were the most important factors. Older cars and cars with higher mileage usually kept less value.

What the project taught

Cleaning the data and matching MSRP are just as important as training the models. A wrong MSRP changes the answer we are trying to predict. More complicated models are not always much better, and adding text features does not always help.

Challenges

Some websites blocked scraping. The two usable sites had different column names and missing details. Cars also appeared more than once. Matching trim names was difficult, and 292 unique cars were removed because MSRP could not be matched clearly.

Limitations and next steps

The data covers only nine models from selected search pages at one point in time. It does not represent the whole market. Listing prices are asking prices, not final sale prices. Original MSRP is the trim's starting price and may miss options or fees. Inflation is not included.

Condition, accidents and maintenance are not fully measured. The test uses cars from the same snapshot and websites as training, so future results may be different. A next step would be collecting later listings and adding reliable condition and history information.
