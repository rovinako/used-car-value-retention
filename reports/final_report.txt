Used Car Value Retention

Rovena Nako | October 23, 2026

1. Project description

This project looks at how much value a used car keeps. The goal is to predict its value and see what affects it most. Value retention = listing price / original MSRP. A ratio of 0.60 means the asking price is 60% of the original MSRP.

2. Data collection

The listings came from CarGurus and iSeeCars. Requests and BeautifulSoup were used to scrape them. MSRP prices came from CarGurus Research. The links and dates are saved with the data.

Source | Raw rows | Unique before merge | Final rows
CarGurus | 1824 | 887 | 884
iSeeCars | 1080 | 736 | 441
The final dataset has 1,325 unique cars from 2015-2025. It includes Toyota (Camry, Corolla, RAV4), Honda (Accord, Civic, CR-V), and Ford (Fusion, Escape, Explorer). Fusion years end at 2020. Each source had more than 500 unique valid listings before merging.

Collected fields include VIN, listing ID, make, model, year, trim, price, mileage, body type, color, title and location. That gives more than ten original fields.

3. Cleaning and combining the data

The column names were changed so both files could be combined. Prices are in dollars and mileage is in miles. The code checks VINs, prices ($1,000-$150,000) and mileage (0-500,000). Repeated cars are removed using VIN. When a car is on both sites, the CarGurus row is kept because it has more details.

Step | Rows left

Selected makes, models and years 2015-2025 | 2420
Valid price, mileage and VIN | 2420

Each car is matched to MSRP using make, model, year and trim. Cars without a clear match are left out. For training, missing numbers use the training median and missing categories use 'unknown'.

4. Feature engineering

Age is calculated by subtracting the model year from the collection year. Miles per year is also saved, but the models use age and mileage separately.

Two new columns check whether the listing mentions leather or navigation: 154 leather and 253 navigation mentions. Text is available for 881 cars. These are short equipment descriptions. Missing text stays unknown. Another column shows whether a description was available.

One-hot encoding makes a 0/1 column for each category. The training data has 561 cities and 91 trims. Categories seen fewer than ten times are grouped together. This keeps the number of columns smaller, but loses some detail.

5. Models

All three models use the same 80/20 split: 1060 training cars and 265 test cars. Each make/model is included in both sets, using random_state=42. Three-fold cross-validation compares models within the training set. Filling missing values, scaling and encoding use training data only.

Model | Settings
Ridge regression | alpha=10
Random forest | 200 trees, min_samples_leaf=3, max_features=0.8
Gradient boosting | 150 trees, learning_rate=0.05, max_depth=3, min_samples_leaf=5
Both tree models use seed 42. Inputs are age, mileage, MSRP, text columns, make, model, trim, body type, color, city and source. Numbers are scaled to a similar range. Listing price and retention are left out because they would give away the answer.

6. Results

Model | CV RMSE | Test MAE | Test RMSE | Test R²
Ridge regression | 0.0619 | 0.0474 | 0.0612 | 0.9091
Random forest | 0.0651 | 0.0507 | 0.0649 | 0.8979
Gradient boosting | 0.0615 | 0.0472 | 0.0602 | 0.9122
MAE is the average error. RMSE gives more weight to large errors. Lower is better for both. Higher R² is better; it is not accuracy. Gradient boosting had the lowest training CV error. Its test MAE of 0.0472 means an average error of about 4.72 percentage points of original value. Ridge was close, so a simpler model also worked well.

8. What affects car value?

To check which features mattered, one column was shuffled and the model was tested again. This was done ten times. If the error went up a lot, that feature was important.

Figure 5. Mileage, make and age were the main predictors. Bars show the average change in RMSE; error lines show variation across shuffles.

Older cars and cars with more miles usually kept less value. Make also mattered. But the brands had different ages and mileage, so the difference cannot be explained by brand alone.

MSRP and city helped a little. Leather and navigation mentions did not add much. Many descriptions were missing, and trim or MSRP may already explain these differences. The ranking shows what the model used, not proof of cause and effect.

Figure 6. The model's average prediction when age or mileage changes. These show model patterns, not how one specific car will lose value.

9. Conclusion

The project used 1,325 unique cars from two websites and compared three models. Gradient boosting performed best in this experiment, with ridge close behind. Mileage, make and age were the most important factors. Older cars and cars with higher mileage usually kept less value.

What this project shows

The main lesson is that clean data matters. If MSRP is wrong, the value retention is wrong too. A complicated model is not always much better than a simple one.

Challenges

Some websites blocked scraping. The two sites used different names for columns, had missing details and listed some cars more than once. Trim names were hard to match, so 292 unique cars were removed because MSRP could not be matched clearly.

What could be better

The data only covers nine models and one collection period. It does not cover the whole market. Asking prices may differ from sale prices. MSRP may miss extra options and fees, and inflation is not included.

Condition, accidents and maintenance are not fully known. The test cars came from the same websites and period as the training cars. More recent listings and better car-history data would help check whether the results hold up.
